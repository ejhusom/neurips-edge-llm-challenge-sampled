Sustainable LLM Inference for Edge AI

We ran 28 compressed versions of four small language models on a Raspberry Pi 4 and measured the power behind every answer with a lab-grade meter. Shrinking a model saved up to 79% of the energy per token. What it cost in accuracy depended on the task.

Setup

One small computer, one power meter, 26,251 answers

Language models usually run in data centres. Running them on small devices close to where data is produced (a sensor hub, a robot, a vehicle) keeps data local and avoids network delays. But these devices have little memory and a tight power budget, so we measured what running a model on one actually costs.

Every model ran on a Raspberry Pi 4 with 4 GB of memory using the Ollama runtime. A Joulescope JS110 power meter sat between the Pi and its power supply and logged the accumulated energy twice per second. A joule is one watt for one second. Before counting an answer's energy we subtracted the Pi's idle draw of 2.85 W, so the numbers reflect the work of answering and nothing else.

Five kinds of questions

The questions come from five public benchmarks, chosen for the NeurIPS 2024 Edge-Device LLM Competition. We used a sample of 200 questions from each, and all 164 HumanEval problems.

Background

Quantization: storing a model with fewer bits

A language model is mostly a very long list of numbers called weights. Llama 3.2 1B has 1.2 billion of them. At full 16-bit precision (FP16) each weight takes 16 bits. Quantization rounds the weights to a small set of values so each fits in 8, 4 or even 3 bits. The file shrinks, less data moves through memory for every word the model writes, and the model fits on hardware that could not otherwise hold it. The price is rounding error.

Try it on a block of 32 example weights. Fewer bits leave fewer values to round to. The formats used by Ollama (from the llama.cpp project) limit the damage by giving each small block of weights its own scale.

RQ1 How does quantization affect energy use?

Quantization cut energy per token by up to 79%

Averaged over the five tasks, full-precision Llama 3.2 1B used 17.60 joules for every token it generated. Its most compressed version, q3_K_S, used 3.75. Fewer bits usually meant less energy, but not always.

The replay shows something the averages hide: on a Raspberry Pi, most of the time goes into reading the question rather than writing the answer.

Long answers are cheaper per token

Energy per token varied six-fold between tasks: 11.92 J on BIG-Bench Hard and 1.94 J on HumanEval. HumanEval answers are code, 162 tokens on average against 8 to 22 for the other tasks. Every question comes with a fixed cost of reading it, and a long answer spreads that cost over more tokens.

RQ2 What does the energy saving cost in accuracy?

Accuracy depended more on the task than on the bits

Quantization affected accuracy unevenly. Some 3-bit versions held up well; others collapsed on particular tasks. Math was hard for every model, whatever its precision.

RQ3 How does quantization affect speed?

Quantized models answered up to 69% faster

Waiting time matters on small devices too. Moving Qwen 2.5 0.5B from FP16 to 8-bit cut the average wait from 31.4 to 9.8 seconds per answer, and Llama 3.2 1B went from 59.7 to 26.3 seconds. Below 8 bits the gains flattened out.

Seconds per answer
Dot: mean. Bar: middle 50% of answers (single tasks only). Lower is better.

Parameter count is not the whole story. Qwen 2.5 1.5B averaged 33.9 seconds per answer, a little faster than the smaller Llama 3.2 1B at 36.3. And the task matters as much as the model: HumanEval answers took 94.8 seconds on average, against 14 to 33 seconds for the other tasks, because the answers are so much longer.

Beyond the Pi 4

The pattern held on a Raspberry Pi 5

We repeated the energy measurements on a Raspberry Pi 5 with 8 GB of memory, using a USB power meter with a lower sampling rate. Absolute numbers shift, because the Pi 5 draws more power, but the broad picture stayed the same: the small Qwen model was cheapest, 8-bit versions were cheapest on average (47 J per answer) and 3-bit versions the most expensive (157 J).

A quick check with one model on a Pi 4 with 8 GB matched the 4 GB results closely: 0.54 against 0.62 J per token on HumanEval.

About the study

Limits, and how to cite

One main deviceThe measurements come from a Raspberry Pi 4 (4 GB), with a smaller check on a Pi 5. Other edge hardware, such as Jetson boards, Coral accelerators or phones, may behave differently.
One family of quantization methodsWe tested weight-only post-training quantization as implemented in llama.cpp through Ollama. Quantization-aware training and methods like AWQ were outside the scope.
Sampled benchmarksEach task used 200 questions (164 for HumanEval), and results vary a lot from question to question, so read small differences between models with care.
Whole-device measurementEnergy is for the whole Pi minus its idle power, logged twice per second. Background activity and temperature add some noise, and answers were stopped after 20 minutes.
